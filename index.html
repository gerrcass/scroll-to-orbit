01WHY WE FLY

Orbit is not
a destination.

Space is the next place we do our most careful work. Most of what orbit does for us is invisible: the storm spotted early, the ship routed around it, the field measured a little more honestly.

HALCYON builds the vehicle that puts those instruments there — and brings the first stage home, again and again. Kestrel is sized for payloads that do not need a spectacle. It is built for cadence: load, launch, land, inspect, repeat.

The night shift is not a metaphor. It is a schedule. Our customers fly when the data is needed, not when the cameras are ready.

04ASCENT

The first minute
is the loudest.

Kestrel leaves the pad with a thrust-to-weight ratio of 1.32 — enough to go, not enough to waste. Max-Q arrives at 11.8 kilometres, where the atmosphere is still thick enough to punish every unnecessary kilogram.

MAX-Q / 11.8 KM

The moment the vehicle meets the most aerodynamic pressure it will ever see. Throttle rolls back; the structure holds; the sky gets quieter.

05SEPARATION

Let go of
what got you here.

The first stage has done its work. It separates, flips, and begins the long fall home while the second stage lights for the last push. Then the fairing opens, and the payload sees the dark for the first time.

  1. 01
    MECO

    Nine engines shut down in sequence. The vehicle coasts for three seconds.

  2. 02
    STAGE SEP

    Pneumatic pushers release the booster. It flips, re-lights, and turns home.

  3. 03
    FAIRING SEP

    The two halves peel away on spring hinges, 118 seconds after liftoff.

  4. 04
    DEPLOY

    The satellite stack is released into its dawn-to-dusk orbit.

06ORBITAL INSERTION

And then,
the quiet returns.
